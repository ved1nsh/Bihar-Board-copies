(a) Value of Education

Education holds immense value in shaping individuals and societies. It serves as a cornerstone for personal growth and societal progress. Firstly, education empowers individuals with knowledge and skills, opening doors to various opportunities. It enhances critical thinking, problem-solving and communication skills, which are essential for navigating the complexities of life.

Moreover, education fosters social development by promoting values such as tolerance, respect and cooperation. It plays a pivotal role in creating informed and responsible citizens who contribute positively to their communities. Education is a catalyst for social mobility, breaking the chains of poverty and inequality.

On an economic level, a well-educated populace drives innovation and economic prosperity. It equips individuals with the expertise required for a rapidly evolving job market, enhancing their employability and contributing to overall economic growth.

Furthermore, education promotes cultural understanding and global cooperation. It bridges gaps between different cultures, fostering a sense of unity and shared humanity.

In conclusion, the value of education extends far beyond individual achievements. It is the key to personal fulfillment, societal advancement, economic prosperity and global harmony. Investing in education is an investment in a brighter and more equitable future.

(a) 'He hath no desire, nor sense, / Nor halfe so short a way; / Then feare not mee,'

This stanza has been taken from the poem 'Sweetest Love, I Do Not Goe' written by John Donne. In this poem the poet, like a lover, consoles his beloved and expresses his deep love for her while leaving for a journey. He compares himself with the sun, saying that the sun has no desire or sense, yet it travels and returns. The poet, who has both desire and sense and a much shorter way to travel, will surely return faster. So he tells his beloved not to fear his parting.

(a) What does Pearl S. Buck mean by saying 'Religion is ever present in Indian life'?

The author wanted to express the role of religion in Indian life. She gives an example: while she was sitting in her host's room, a gentleman came in and, without speaking, moved to the far end of the room, knelt with his head bowed, and prayed for about a quarter of an hour. Her hostess explained that he was her husband's eldest brother, offering prayer. By this, the author means that religion is present in every walk of an Indian's life.

(b) What did the doctor advise Benjy's parents to ensure his mental growth?

(c) What do you mean by the term 'free press'?

By 'free press' we usually mean freedom of the press in a technical and restricted sense — namely, freedom from direction or censorship by the government. In this respect, the British press, under ordinary conditions, is singularly free.

(d) How are the children of the joint family in Bangladesh looked after?

In Bangladesh, children under the age of five or six are looked after by the whole family. All the children of the joint family are looked after together. They are taken to the pond for a bath, perhaps by one daughter-in-law, who bathes them all. Then they all come in and sit down to eat together.

Ans

(a) Summary of the poem 'The Soldier'

'The Soldier' is a patriotic sonnet written by Rupert Brooke. In this poem the soldier expresses his deep love for his country, England. He says that if he dies in a foreign land during the war, then that corner of the foreign field should be thought of as forever England. His body, made of English dust, will enrich the foreign soil. England gave him birth, shaped him, gave him flowers to love and air to breathe. Even after death, his soul will give back to England all the beautiful thoughts and joys it once received. The poem is full of praise and love for England and shows the soldier's pride in dying for his motherland.

(b) Summary of the prose-piece 'I Have a Dream'

'I Have a Dream' is a famous speech by Martin Luther King Jr. In it he speaks of his dream of a free and equal America, where people are judged not by the colour of their skin but by their character. He reminds his listeners that even after the abolition of slavery, the Negroes still suffer from discrimination and injustice. He urges them to continue their struggle peacefully, without hatred or violence, working together with love and faith. He dreams of a day when people of all races will live together as brothers and sisters with full justice and freedom. The speech is a powerful call for equality and human dignity.

(c) A note on Indian English

Indian English refers to the variety of the English language used in India. The English language came to India with the British and, over time, became one of the important languages of the country. Indian English has its own special features in pronunciation, vocabulary and sentence structure, often influenced by Indian languages like Hindi. Many Indian words (such as 'guru', 'bungalow', 'jungle') have entered standard English. Today, English is widely used in India in education, administration, courts, business and the media, and serves as a link language between people of different regions. The Anglo-Indian community even uses English as its mother tongue. Thus, Indian English has become a recognised and living form of the language.
