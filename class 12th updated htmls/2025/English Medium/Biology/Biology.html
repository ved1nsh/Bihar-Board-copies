What is morula?
Ans

The morula is an early stage of embryonic development consisting of a solid spherical mass of 16–32 blastomeres formed by repeated mitotic cleavage of the zygote, without any internal cavity. It looks like a tiny mulberry (hence the name from Latin morum = mulberry) and is the stage just preceding the blastula (blastocyst). In humans the morula stage is attained roughly 3–4 days after fertilisation, while the embryo is still travelling down the fallopian tube toward the uterus.

Any two benefits of self-pollination.
Ans

(i) Purity of progeny: Self-pollination preserves the parental genotype and produces pure-line offspring, so desirable agronomic characters are maintained generation after generation without segregation.

(ii) Pollination is assured: The plant does not depend on external agents (wind, water, insects); pollen wastage is minimum and seed set is almost guaranteed even when pollinators are absent or the population is sparse.

What do you mean by Camouflage?
Ans

Camouflage is a protective adaptation by which an organism so resembles its background — in colour, pattern, shape or texture — that it becomes nearly indistinguishable from its surroundings. It helps prey to avoid being detected by predators and helps predators to approach prey unnoticed, thus enhancing survival. Common examples are the leaf-like wings of the dead-leaf butterfly (Kallima), the stick insect, the chameleon's colour change and the white winter coat of the Arctic fox.

Importance of micro-organisms in research.
Ans

Microorganisms are indispensable tools in modern biological research. They serve as model systems (E. coli, yeast) for studying basic cellular processes such as DNA replication, gene expression and metabolic pathways because they are easy to culture, multiply rapidly and have small, well-mapped genomes. They are also the workhorses of genetic engineering and biotechnology — used as host cells for recombinant DNA, in production of insulin, vaccines, antibiotics, enzymes, vitamins and biofuels, and in bioremediation and environmental monitoring.

Any two advantages of genetically modified animals.
Ans

(i) Production of valuable biological products: Transgenic animals can serve as "bioreactors" — e.g. transgenic cows and goats secrete human pharmaceutical proteins (such as human α-1-antitrypsin or insulin) into their milk, providing cheap and abundant therapeutics.

(ii) Disease models and improved livestock: GM animals (e.g. knockout mice) act as models for human diseases like Alzheimer's, cancer and cystic fibrosis, helping in drug testing; they can also be engineered for higher milk yield, faster growth, leaner meat or disease resistance, improving food security.

What is Klinefelter syndrome?
Ans

Klinefelter syndrome is a sex-chromosomal disorder in human males caused by the presence of an extra X chromosome, giving the karyotype 47, XXY (44 + XXY). It arises by non-disjunction of sex chromosomes during gametogenesis. Affected individuals are phenotypically male but show feminised features — gynaecomastia (enlarged breasts), sparse body and facial hair, small testes, low testosterone and sterility due to absence of sperm production. Mild learning difficulties may also be present.

Two differences between ontogeny and phylogeny.
Ans

(i) Scope: Ontogeny is the developmental history of a single individual from zygote to adult, whereas phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a race or species over geological time.

(ii) Time scale and study: Ontogeny operates over the lifetime of one organism and is studied through embryology; phylogeny operates over millions of years and is reconstructed through fossils, comparative anatomy and molecular data. The biogenetic law states "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny" (Haeckel).

Any two symptoms of sickle cell anaemia.
Ans

(i) Sickle-shaped RBCs and chronic anaemia: Substitution of valine for glutamic acid at position 6 of the β-globin chain causes haemoglobin to polymerise under low O2, distorting erythrocytes into a rigid sickle shape; these cells are rapidly haemolysed, producing severe anaemia, pallor, weakness and breathlessness.

(ii) Vaso-occlusive crises: Sickled cells block small blood vessels, causing severe episodic pain in bones, joints and abdomen, along with jaundice, splenomegaly and increased susceptibility to infections.

What are sacred groves?
Ans

Sacred groves are tracts of virgin forest, including all plants and animals, that have been traditionally protected by local communities on religious or cultural grounds — usually dedicated to a local deity or ancestral spirit. Felling trees and killing wildlife within them is taboo. They form important in-situ refugia of biodiversity, often harbouring rare and endemic species. Well-known examples in India include the sacred groves of Khasi and Jaintia hills (Meghalaya), Western Ghats, Aravallis and the Sarguja-Chanda-Bastar belt.

Define commensalism with two examples.
Ans

Commensalism is an interspecific interaction in which one species (the commensal) is benefited — obtaining shelter, support, food or transport — while the other (the host) is neither benefited nor harmed.

Examples: (i) Epiphytic orchids growing on the branches of a large tree get sunlight and anchorage without harming the tree; (ii) The remora fish attached to a shark gets food scraps and free transport while the shark is unaffected. (Cattle egret feeding on insects flushed up by grazing cattle is another standard example.)

Define germplasm.
Ans

Germplasm is the sum total of hereditary material (genes) of a species — i.e. all the alleles present in the gametes (or in seeds, pollen, tissue cultures, whole plants/animals) that can be used in breeding and conservation. It represents the genetic reservoir of a species. The term was originally introduced by August Weismann (1893) in his "germplasm theory" of heredity. Germplasm is preserved in ex-situ facilities such as gene banks, seed banks, cryobanks, botanical gardens and zoos to safeguard genetic diversity for future use.

What is Gene library?
Ans

A gene library (DNA library) is a collection of cloned DNA fragments, ideally representing the entire genome (or the entire mRNA population) of an organism, stored in a suitable vector inside host cells. There are two main kinds: (i) a genomic library, made from fragments of total genomic DNA cut by restriction enzymes and inserted into vectors such as plasmids, BACs or phages; and (ii) a cDNA library, made by reverse-transcribing the mRNA pool, which represents only the expressed genes. Gene libraries are used to isolate and study specific genes.

Any two harmful effects of sewage.
Ans

(i) Water pollution and eutrophication: Untreated sewage discharged into rivers and lakes is rich in organic matter and nutrients (N, P). It raises BOD, depletes dissolved oxygen, causes algal blooms and eutrophication, and ultimately kills fish and other aquatic life.

(ii) Spread of water-borne diseases: Sewage carries pathogenic bacteria, viruses and parasites (e.g. Vibrio cholerae, Salmonella typhi, hepatitis-A virus, helminth eggs), contaminating drinking water and causing cholera, typhoid, dysentery, jaundice and gastroenteritis.

What is mycorrhiza?
Ans

Mycorrhiza is a mutualistic symbiotic association between certain soil fungi and the roots of higher plants. The fungus (commonly Glomeromycota or Basidiomycota) gets organic carbon (sugars) from the plant, while the plant receives much-improved absorption of water and mineral nutrients (especially phosphorus, zinc and copper) through the vast fungal hyphal network, along with greater drought and pathogen resistance. The two main types are ectomycorrhiza (fungus on root surface, common in pines) and endomycorrhiza/VAM (fungus inside cortex cells, common in crops).

Importance of Red Data Book.
Ans

The Red Data Book (Red List), maintained by the IUCN, is an authoritative catalogue of the conservation status of plant and animal species worldwide. It classifies species into categories such as Extinct, Extinct in the Wild, Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened and Least Concern. It is important because it (i) alerts governments and scientists to species at risk and helps prioritise conservation efforts, (ii) supports planning of protected areas and recovery programmes, and (iii) provides legal/policy backing for laws against trade and exploitation of threatened species.

Two differences between integument and testa.
Ans

(i) Origin and stage: Integument is the protective covering of the ovule (pre-fertilisation), arising from the chalazal region of the nucellus; testa is the protective covering of the seed (post-fertilisation), formed from the outer integument after fertilisation.

Two differences between c-DNA and s-DNA.
Ans

(i) Origin: cDNA (complementary DNA) is synthesised in vitro from a mature mRNA template by the enzyme reverse transcriptase, so it represents only the expressed (transcribed) sequence of a gene. sDNA (satellite DNA) is a naturally occurring class of highly repetitive, non-coding DNA sequences present in the eukaryotic genome.

(ii) Function: cDNA contains only the coding (exonic) sequence and is used in cloning expressed genes and constructing cDNA libraries. Satellite DNA does not code for any protein; because of its highly repetitive and polymorphic nature it forms the basis of DNA fingerprinting.

What is epistasis?
Ans

Epistasis is a form of gene interaction between two non-allelic genes in which one gene (the epistatic gene) masks, modifies or suppresses the phenotypic expression of another gene (the hypostatic gene) located at a different locus. It alters the classical Mendelian dihybrid 9:3:3:1 ratio into modified ratios such as 12:3:1 (dominant epistasis), 9:3:4 (recessive epistasis) or 9:7 (complementary genes). A classical example is coat colour in Labrador retrievers, where genotype ee at the E locus prevents deposition of pigment irrespective of the B locus.

What do you understand by ground water? Describe causes for its depletion and remedies.
Ans

Ground water: Ground water is the fresh water that occurs below the earth's surface within the pores, fractures and cavities of soil, sand, gravel and rock formations. It collects in saturated layers called aquifers and is recharged mainly by infiltration of rain, melting snow, rivers and lakes. It is tapped through wells, tube-wells and natural springs and is the principal source of drinking water and irrigation in India.

Causes of depletion:

• Over-extraction by deep tube-wells for irrigation (especially of paddy, sugarcane) and growing urban & industrial demand far exceeds the natural recharge.

• Deforestation and loss of green cover reduce infiltration and increase surface runoff.

• Rapid urbanisation and concretisation (roads, buildings, pavements) seal recharge zones.

• Pollution of surface and ground water by sewage, industrial effluent and agro-chemicals renders the available reserve unusable.

• Erratic rainfall and climate change reduce monsoon recharge in many regions.

Remedies:

• Rain-water harvesting at household, community and urban scale; rooftop harvesting and recharge wells.

• Watershed management — construction of check-dams, percolation tanks, contour bunds, restoration of village ponds and wetlands.

• Afforestation and protection of catchment areas to enhance infiltration.

• Efficient irrigation (drip, sprinkler), crop diversification away from water-intensive crops, and metering of agricultural electricity.

• Strict regulation of new tube-wells, treatment of effluents before discharge, and public awareness about water conservation.

Differences between any two of the following: (a) Food chain and food web; (b) Primary producer and Secondary producer; (c) Aestivation and Hibernation.
Ans

(a) Food chain vs Food web:

• A food chain is a linear sequence of organisms through which food energy passes from producers to successive trophic levels (e.g. grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk). A food web is the interconnected network of many food chains in an ecosystem, where one organism feeds on, and is fed upon by, several others.

• A food chain has a single, fixed path of energy flow; a food web has multiple alternative pathways, making the ecosystem more stable.

• Removal of any link breaks a simple food chain, but a food web absorbs the loss because alternative food sources are available.

(b) Primary producer vs Secondary producer:

• Primary producers (autotrophs) synthesise organic food from inorganic substances using sunlight (green plants, algae, cyanobacteria) or chemicals (chemosynthetic bacteria). They occupy the first trophic level.

• A secondary producer is, in ecological usage, often equated with primary consumers/herbivores that convert plant biomass into animal biomass available to higher trophic levels (e.g. cow, deer); they cannot synthesise their own food and rely entirely on primary producers.

• Energy efficiency of primary production is higher; secondary production loses energy as heat and respiration (~10 % rule).

(c) Aestivation vs Hibernation:

• Aestivation is summer sleep — a state of dormancy during hot, dry conditions to escape desiccation (e.g. snails, lungfish, frogs in dried-up ponds).

• Hibernation is winter sleep — a state of reduced metabolism in cold conditions to survive low temperature and food scarcity (e.g. bear, ground squirrel, bat).

• In aestivation the animal usually burrows in damp mud; in hibernation it occupies a burrow or den with low body temperature, slow heartbeat and depressed respiration.

Process of sex-determination in honeybees.
Ans

Sex determination in honeybees (Apis mellifera) follows the haplodiploid mechanism, also called the haplo-diploid (or arrhenotokous) sex determination system. It depends on the chromosome set inherited by the egg, not on heteromorphic sex chromosomes.

• A honeybee colony consists of three castes — a fertile queen, sterile worker females and fertile drones (males).

• The queen is diploid (2n = 32) and produces haploid eggs (n = 16) by meiosis. Drones are haploid (n = 16) and produce sperms by mitosis (so all sperms are genetically identical).

• Fertilised eggs (haploid egg + haploid sperm = diploid, 2n = 32) develop into females — either workers (if fed normal royal jelly briefly) or queens (if fed royal jelly continuously). Sex is therefore controlled by ploidy, with the complementary sex-determiner (csd) gene at a single locus: heterozygotes at csd develop as fertile females, while hemizygotes (haploid) develop as males.

• Unfertilised eggs develop parthenogenetically into haploid drones (n = 16). Thus drones have only a mother and no father, but they do have a maternal grandfather.

• Implications: Drones have only half the genome of females, and worker sisters share on average 75 % of their genes (because they all receive identical paternal sperms) — this is the genetic basis of kin selection and altruistic behaviour of workers towards the queen.

• In summary, in honeybees the queen controls the sex of the offspring by deciding whether or not the egg passing through her oviduct will be fertilised by sperms stored in her spermatheca.

Labelled diagram of recombinant DNA technology.
Ans

Recombinant DNA Technology (genetic engineering) is the technique of joining a desired DNA fragment from one organism with the DNA of a vector and transferring it into a host cell to produce useful proteins or modify traits. The flow-diagram with labels is described below:

Step 1 — Isolation of DNA: Total DNA is extracted from the donor cell (e.g. human cell carrying the insulin gene) using enzymes such as lysozyme/proteinase K and purified by precipitation with chilled ethanol.

Step 2 — Cutting of DNA by restriction enzymes: A specific restriction endonuclease (e.g. EcoRI) cuts the donor DNA and the vector DNA (plasmid such as pBR322) at identical recognition sites, producing complementary sticky ends.

Step 3 — Insertion into a vector: The desired fragment is joined to the linearised plasmid by DNA ligase, producing a recombinant DNA (rDNA) molecule.

Step 4 — Transformation: The rDNA is introduced into a competent host cell (commonly E. coli) by treatment with CaCl2 and a brief heat shock, or by electroporation.

Step 5 — Selection of transformants: Host cells carrying the recombinant plasmid are identified using selectable markers (antibiotic resistance genes, blue–white screening with lacZ).

Step 6 — Cloning and expression: Selected cells are grown in a bioreactor; the foreign gene is expressed, and the recombinant protein (e.g. humulin/insulin) is harvested and purified for use.

The labelled diagram should show: donor cell → DNA isolation → restriction enzyme cuts at recognition sites of donor DNA and vector plasmid → sticky ends joined by DNA ligase to form recombinant DNA → uptake by host bacterium → selection of transformed colonies → mass culture → product (e.g. human insulin).

Describe biofortification in brief.
Ans

Biofortification is the process of breeding or genetically engineering crops with higher levels of essential vitamins, minerals, proteins, healthier fats and other nutrients, so that everyday staple foods themselves become a sustainable means of combating "hidden hunger" (micronutrient deficiency) in human populations. It is one of the most practical and important goals of modern plant breeding and biotechnology, especially in developing countries.

Objectives:

• Higher protein content and improved amino-acid balance (e.g. lysine, tryptophan).

• Higher content of vitamins, especially Vitamin A, B-complex and C.

• Higher mineral content — iron, zinc, iodine, calcium.

• Better quality of oil and reduction of anti-nutrients.

Methods:

• Conventional plant breeding — selection and crossing of varieties having naturally high micronutrients.

• Agronomic biofortification — foliar or soil application of fertilisers rich in target micronutrient (e.g. Zn).

• Transgenic / genetic-engineering approaches — introducing genes that boost nutrient biosynthesis.

Famous examples:

• Golden Rice — rice engineered to accumulate β-carotene (provitamin A) in the endosperm using psy (daffodil/maize) and crtI (bacterial) genes, intended to combat Vitamin-A deficiency-related blindness.

• Atlas 66 wheat, having high protein content, has been used as a donor for improving cultivated wheat. An iron-fortified rice variety has also been developed.

• Maize hybrids with twice the amount of the amino acids lysine and tryptophan compared to existing maize hybrids have been developed.

• The Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI), New Delhi, has released several vegetable crops rich in vitamins and minerals — vitamin A enriched carrots, spinach and pumpkin; vitamin C enriched bitter gourd, bathua, mustard and tomato; iron and calcium enriched spinach and bathua; and protein-enriched broad, lablab, French and garden beans.

Significance: Biofortification is a cost-effective, sustainable strategy that delivers nutrition through staple foods to large populations without altering food habits, and complements other approaches like dietary diversification, food fortification and nutrient supplementation.
